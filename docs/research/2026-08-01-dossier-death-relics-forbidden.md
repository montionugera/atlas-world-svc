| **Pollution doctrine (miasma):** an unburied or improperly buried corpse was believed to emit a contagion that attached to the family, the household and the whole city, not just the individual                                                                                                                                         | Priesthoods and purification specialists, who sold the remedy; also the city, which got a cheap enforcement lever on private families | Purification rites, delay of ordinary business, exclusion of the polluted from temples and assemblies                                                              | Pollution was politically weaponised — accusing a rival lineage of inherited blood-guilt disqualified them from office                                                                                                     | Pollution is **transmissible and inherited**, not personal: it moves from a corpse to a house to a bloodline to a city. | **Capital liability of commanders for un-recovered war dead:** after Arginusae (406 BC) eight Athenian generals were deposed for failing to recover survivors and the dead from 25 sunk ships; six returned and were tried collectively and executed                                                                                     | Prosecuting politicians (Callixeinus and allies); the bereaved families, who got redress                                              | Six senior commanders executed after a naval **victory**; Athens later regretted it and charged the instigators, who fled                                          | The vote was pushed through as a single collective verdict, which the assembly president refused to put as unconstitutional. | **Licensed funeral monopoly (lex libitinaria, Puteoli, Augustan era):** one contractor held the exclusive right to all funerals, corpse removal and public punishments in the town, with tariffs carved on a stone at least 2.5 m wide for public inspection                                                                             | The contractor (a private monopolist); the town, which offloaded corpse-disposal and executions onto one accountable party            | Every citizen, including councillors, was obliged to buy from him; residents were forbidden to leave a corpse unburied; **60 sesterces fine per abandoned corpse** | The same firm that buried you also flogged and executed people; the monopoly bundled grief and violence into one contract                                                                                                  | Tariff detail: **4 sesterces** per yoke-carrying workman, per flogger, and per executioner for punishing a private slave; public executions supplied free. | **Segregation and marking of corpse-handlers (same law):** the contractor's workmen were barred from living or washing in the town's tower precinct from the first hour of night, barred from entering the city except to carry a body or perform a punishment, and required to wear **a multi-coloured cap** when inside                | The town's ritual cleanliness; the contractor, who got a captive workforce nobody else would hire                                     | Permanent social exclusion of ~32+ men; hiring rules excluded the disabled, the tattooed and men outside a set age band                                            | A workforce defined by disqualification is a workforce with nowhere else to go — the exclusion is what keeps wages payable                                                                                                 | The **coloured cap is a uniform of untouchability**: not a badge of office, a visibility requirement so citizens can avoid you on the street                                                                                                                                                            | [Ostia-Antica.org: Puteoli — the undertakers](http://www.ostia-antica.org/dict/topics/puteoli/presentation/puteoli-undertakers.htm)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| **Hereditary corpse-bearer caste (Zoroastrian _nasesalar_ / _khandhia_):** dead matter is ritually polluting; a specialised class carries the body and enters the tower of exposure, and lives apart from the rest of the community                                                                                                      | The community, whose ordinary members never touch a corpse; the priesthood, which owns the purification rites                         | Corpse-bearers hold permanently low ritual status; anyone who touches a body must undergo a purificatory bath before rejoining normal life                         | Status is attached to the _occupation_, not to any act of wrongdoing — the pollution is structural and non-negotiable                                                                                                      | Two grades exist: those who only give shoulder to the bier and those who may enter the exposure tower after extra ritual precaution. **Even among the untouchable there is a rank**                                                                                                                     | [Ramiyar Karanjia, Q&A on death and dokhmenashini](https://ramiyarkaranjia.com/16-qa-about-death-and-dokhmenashini/); [Tower of Silence overview](https://www.lastjourney.in/blog/what-is-the-zoroastrian-tower-of-silence/)                                                                                                                                                  |

| **Denial of burial as an ordinary legal penalty:** Lateran IV canon 21 (1215) attaches denial of Christian burial to failure to confess and take Easter communion annually                                                                                                                                                               | The church, which gained a sanction cheaper than force and enforceable on the _survivors_                                             | The penalty lands on the dead person's family and reputation, not on the offender's body                                                                           | Exclusion from the churchyard drove demand for unconsecrated and improvised burial, and for expensive posthumous rehabilitation                                                                                            | The sanction is **applied after death and paid by the living**. The bureaucratic step is the bottleneck, not the digging                                                                                                                                                       | [Medieval Soldier: burials of the Agincourt dead](https://medievalsoldier.org/about/agincourt/the-french-army-in-1415/burials-of-agincourt-dead/); [Soton ePrints: burials](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/400374/3/Burials.htm); [Where are the dead of medieval battles? _J. | **Failure of the salvage economy under time pressure (Visby, 27 July 1361):** ~1,800 Gotlanders killed; three mass graves later yielded 1,185 individuals — many buried still wearing their mail and coats of plates                                                                                                                     | Archaeologists, six centuries later — the single richest medieval armour assemblage known                                             | Contemporary looters lost the armour, which was the most valuable thing on the field                                                                               | Keegan's reading: **hot weather plus sheer numbers defeated the effort to strip them before decomposition began** (attribution to Keegan reported in secondary sources — treat the causal claim as _probable, not proven_) | Decay has a **clock that beats greed**. Past a threshold of heat and body count, valuable loot is abandoned because handling the bodies becomes intolerable                                                                                                                                             | [Battle of Visby, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Visby); Bengt Thordeman, _Armour from the Battle of Wisby, 1361_ (1939)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Late reopening of war graves as a political act (Towton, 1461):** grave pits were reopened in 1484 — 23 years later, under Richard III — for reburial; medieval excavators left behind bones they missed or disregarded, which is what archaeology recovered                                                                           | The reigning king, who bought legitimacy by honouring the dead of a battle he did not fight                                           | A second full exhumation and reinterment, decades after the fact, at royal expense                                                                                 | The reburial was selective: the medieval diggers took some remains and left others in the pit                                                                                                                              | Grave pits stayed **visible as surface features from the 16th to the 18th century**. The scar outlives everyone who could explain it                                                                                                                                                                    | [Towton Battlefield Archaeology Project — human remains](http://towtonbattle.free.fr/index.php/human-remains/); [Blood Red Roses / Towton mass grave (Assemblage)](https://assemblagejournal.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/blood-red-roses.pdf)                                                                                                                    |

| **The parchment label (_cedula_ / "authentic") as the entire provenance system:** a strip of parchment, one example about **90 × 15–22 mm**, tied to the silk bag holding the relic, naming what it is                                                                                                 | The holding institution — the label _is_ the title deed                                            | Trivial materially; enormous institutionally. Roughly seventy labels survive from Chelles alone (7th–8th c.), written by nuns in a house-recognisable script | Lose the label and the relic collapses from "the beard of a named saint" into anonymous bone; a forged label costs one strip of parchment                                                                                                                                                           | Sample texts are terse to the point of absurdity: _rel sci gennouefe_, _de barba sancti bonifatii_ — "from the beard of…"                                                                          | [JHI Blog: accessing the secrets of early medieval relic labels](https://www.jhiblog.org/2015/06/01/accessing-the-secrets-of-early-medieval-relic-labels/)                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| **Seal-and-thread integrity:** the reliquary is closed with threads and a wax seal bearing the issuing authority's insignia; breaking the seal voids the authentication                                                                                                                                | The issuing authority, which becomes the guarantor of every relic it seals                         | Any inspection destroys the proof — you cannot verify without invalidating                                                                                   | The system rewards _never looking_. Nobody has an incentive to open the box                                                                                                                                                                                                                         | **Verification and destruction of the warranty are the same action.** The unopened seal is the asset                                                                                               | [JHI Blog on relic labels](https://www.jhiblog.org/2015/06/01/accessing-the-secrets-of-early-medieval-relic-labels/); [Medieval and early modern methods to authenticate relics (Academia.edu)](https://www.academia.edu/36757747/Medieval_and_early_modern_methods_to_authenticate_relics_docx)                                                                |
| **Non-documentary authentication — ordeal by fire, visions, dreams, divine signs:** where paperwork was absent, authenticity was established by a test the relic itself was said to pass                                                                                                               | Whoever staged the test; the local clergy who narrated the result                                  | The tests are unfalsifiable in practice and were reported by interested parties                                                                              | Medieval writers themselves record scepticism about these methods — the anecdotes exist _because_ people doubted                                                                                                                                                                                    | Authentication is a **public performance with an audience**, not a laboratory procedure. Whoever controls the staging controls the verdict                                                         | [Medieval and early modern methods to authenticate relics (Academia.edu)](https://www.academia.edu/36757747/Medieval_and_early_modern_methods_to_authenticate_relics_docx)                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| **Regulatory licensing of relic display and sale (Lateran IV, 1215, canon 62):** prohibited selling relics, forbade veneration of newly-found relics without papal approval, and required licensed alms-collectors (questors) to carry authenticated papal letters                                     | The papacy — a central registry that certifies value; honest shrines, protected from competitors   | Every new find now needed a slow, distant approval before it could earn                                                                                      | **NB: the canon's exact wording could not be retrieved from an accessible full-text source in this pass** (Fordham and papalencyclicals.net both truncated). Sanctity had a wholesale tier, and the buyers knew the goods were doubtful                                                                  | [Atlas Obscura: the business of bones](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/a-business-of-bones-relic-trafficking-in-the-middle-ages); [Marcellinus and Peter, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcellinus_and_Peter); [Einhard, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einhard)                                                                    |

| **Criminal statute plus discretionary royal licence (England, Act Against Multipliers, 13 Jan 1404):** made alchemical "multiplication" of gold and silver a felony                                                                                                                                                                                                         | The crown — protecting the coinage _and_ holding a monopoly on exceptions                                                      | Practitioners criminalised for the craft itself, not for any fraud committed                                                                               | From **1440** Henry VI, short of money after costly wars, began issuing **special licences** exempting favoured practitioners. ## Sharpest hooks

The five with the most fantasy potential, and why each is not a cliché.

1. **The licensed funeral monopoly that also runs the executions** (Puteoli tariff stone: 60 sesterces per abandoned corpse; 4 sesterces per flogger; coloured caps; curfew on where the workers may sleep).
   _Not a cliché because_ it makes death rites an **infrastructure contract with a published price list**, not a mystical office. The horror is procurement, and the outcast is outcast by clause, not by curse.

2. **Per-inch pricing of corpses** (six shillings the first foot, ninepence the inch; eight guineas average in 1828 against a weaver's five shillings a week).
   _Not a cliché because_ it replaces "grave-robbers are ghouls" with **a commodity market with a grading standard**. Bodies are stock; the trade has quotes, gangs, and a select-committee headcount.

3. **A ban maintained for 285 years so that exemptions could be sold** (Act Against Multipliers 1404 → royal licences from 1440 → repeal 1689).
   _Not a cliché because_ the forbidden art is not suppressed and not secret — it is **illegal-except-by-favour**, so the actual contraband is the permit. Nobody hides in a tower; they queue at a chancery.

4. **The relic whose doctrine makes it unfalsifiable, plus the seal you cannot inspect without voiding** (Hailes: visible only to the already-absolved; cedula 90 × 20 mm; wax seal and thread as the whole warranty).
   _Not a cliché because_ it locates power in **epistemics and paperwork** rather than in the object's magic. Doubt is redirected onto the doubter, and the only verification procedure destroys the thing it verifies.

5. **Decay beating greed on the battlefield** (Visby 1361: ~1,800 dead in July heat, 1,185 recovered, many still in their armour that nobody could face stripping).
   _Not a cliché because_ it is the opposite of the looted-battlefield image: **the most valuable objects in the world are left on the field because the bodies got there first.** A clock, a temperature and a body count, not a haunting.

## Not verified / open

- **Lateran IV canon 62 wording.** Both accessible full-text hosts (Fordham, papalencyclicals.net) truncated before canon 62. Existence and substance confirmed only via secondary summary; the verbatim text should be checked against Tanner's _Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils_ before any claim quotes it.
- **Nilson's Canterbury percentages (88% / 28%).** Reported in a secondary summary, not checked against the book.
- **Murano death penalty for defecting glassmakers.** Repeated everywhere in popular sources, disputed by historians. The 1291 furnace relocation and the travel-authorisation requirement are the safe parts.
- **The Rood of Grace as deliberate fraud.** Actively contested in the scholarship; the mechanism itself is not in doubt, the _deception_ is.
- **Scale of the battlefield bone trade.** Newspaper-attested (1822) and repeated, but archaeologically thin; a 2022 study explicitly reopened the question.
- **Keegan's heat-and-numbers explanation for the Visby armour.** Plausible and widely repeated; the causal claim itself is an interpretation, not an excavation finding.
- **Darnton's smuggling economics** (insurance rates, carrier fees, price per hundredweight over the Jura) could not be retrieved at the level of specific figures in this pass. The route, the euphemism and the best-seller finding are solid; the tariffs would need the book.
